Playbooks and resources leaders actually use

Chapter leaders told us resources were hard to find. I used AI to build one interactive playbook for 27 leadership teams. This demo rebuilds it for a fictional company, PMO Enterprises.

The story

Problem

Leaders couldn't find the resources they needed, so questions came to the regional office, and teams took longer than they should to reach the standard.

Approach

A single web playbook: 13 sections, a live dashboard, a regional calendar and an AI assistant that answers only from playbook content. Piloted confidentially with regional leadership, then rolled out with a signed acknowledgement from every chair.

Result

Every chapter uses it. Management-directed questions are down 30%, and chapters reach the standard faster than ever.

What I'd do differently

Write a test set of real leader questions for the assistant before launch, so I could report its accuracy as well as its use.

What's in the demo

The real playbook is confidential. The demo keeps its structure and features; every name, number and link is invented, and each link opens a sample page showing what you'd find there.

Live dashboard, embedded

Dwell-time analytics

How I build AI workflows

I treat AI like a new team member: it gets the context once, the right tools for the job and access to where the work lives. Then I check its work.

My stack, layer by layer

Each layer removes a step I'd otherwise repeat by hand.

Context

Each workstream has its own project, with the method, rules and history written down, so I never re-explain the job.

Skills

Packaged know-how for a kind of task: building Word reports, PDFs, spreadsheets and slide decks to the same standard every time.

Connectors

Live access to the apps where work lives, such as email, calendars and files, so AI reads the source instead of a copy and paste.

Plugins

Bundles of skills, connectors and commands for a team process, so a workflow I design can be installed by colleagues, not just used by me.

Ship and measure

GitHub and Vercel to publish, a serverless function for the playbook assistant, and Apps Script to log what gets used.

The story

Problem

Health, commitments, engagement, membership and satisfaction lived in four places, on different dates, under different chapter names.

Approach

One self-contained report with the data built in, refreshed monthly, and quarterly for satisfaction. A concern score ranks chapters, and restricted visit notes sit behind a password on their own tab.

Result

Regional chairs get one link before every chapter visit. The same data now feeds board submissions and policy proposals.

What I'd do differently

Put restricted notes behind real server-side access control. The current password is a soft gate, and I'd rather it were a hard one.

Any approved AI tool

  • Your own drafts and notes
  • Templates and brainstorming

Approved tool, minimal detail

Your organisation's enterprise AI

  • Internal documents and notes
  • Client names, removed where you can
  • Unreleased plans

Only with explicit approval and controls

  • Financial, legal or HR matters
  • Anything under NDA or regulation

How I design learning

My Oxford MSc was in how adults learn. For AI, that means building confidence and judgement, not just teaching features.

The 4C framework for AI capability

How I'd move a business from curiosity to self-sustaining capability. People sit at different stages, so each stage gets its own learning, not one course for everyone.

Stage 1

Curious

Has heard the hype, hasn't used AI for real work.

What they need
To see it work on their own task, safely.
What I build
Short live demos on real tasks and a one-page guardrails guide.
How we know
A first real task done within 14 days.
Stage 2

Capable

Uses AI for simple tasks, but not consistently.

What they need
A reliable way to ask, and to check.
What I build
Role-based tutorials, prompt patterns and job aids.
How we know
Weekly use and time saved on routine work.
Stage 3

Confident

Uses AI daily, and knows when not to.

What they need
Workflows built around their real processes.
What I build
Workflow clinics, team templates, and skills and connectors set up for the team.
How we know
A workflow in daily use, with quality checks built in.
Stage 4

Champion

Teaches others and spots new uses.

What they need
Recognition, time and a network.
What I build
Train-the-trainer, a champions network and a shared library.
How we know
Peers trained and new use cases submitted.
Judgement stays human

At every stage, AI drafts and people decide. Learning always covers checking the output, not just producing it.

Guardrails from day one

Everyone knows what can go into which tool before they start, so confidence never outruns safety.

Six principles behind everything I build

  • Start from the behaviour, not the content.I ask what people need to do differently at work, then build only what gets them there.Action mapping
  • Respect what adults already know.Senior people learn when it's relevant, self-directed and built on their experience.Knowles' andragogy
  • Show, then hand over.One worked example, then a partial one they finish, then their own real task.Merrill's first principles
  • Cut anything that doesn't help.Short segments, plain screens, one idea at a time.Mayer's multimedia principles
  • Most learning happens back at the desk.Job aids, champions and office hours do more than any single session.70-20-10
  • Measure what changed, not who attended.Behaviour at 30 days matters more than the rating on the day.Kirkpatrick's four levels

My view on AI

AI is a tool that speeds up tasks. It doesn't replace professional judgement. I don't see it as a threat; I see it as a way to add time to the working day without adding hours.

The thinking that matters most stays with us: what's true, what's right for this person, and what to do next. My job is to help people hand AI the work that slows them down, so they have more time for the work only they can do.

My story

I started in curriculum design, moved into designing learning for chief executives, and now run a region of 27 chapters for a global CEO organisation.

AI isn't in my job description. I took it on because I could see what it would do for the people I work with, and it's become the part of my work I care about most. I've trained colleagues across EMEA and APAC, built the tools my region runs on, and learnt what it takes to move busy, senior people from curious to confident.
